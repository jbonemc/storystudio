# Story Studio method notes (for AI coaching prompts)

Jonathan McCrea's method, digested from his course material. Quoted lines are his wording (dashes converted to commas). Sources: [B] SESSION-BLOCKS.md, [H] Exercise Handbook, [LG] Complete Episode Learning Guides, [KB] Knowledgebase, [CTG] Content Tool Guide, [MT] Media Training Basics. Where sources disagree, both versions are given.

## 1. Core mindset

- "Most of us communicate by default. We say what is on our mind without pausing to consider how it is received." [B]
- "So stop asking 'what do I want to say?' and start asking 'what do I want my audience to think, feel or do, and what language will move them there?' Everything else follows from that one change." [B]
- "communication is not about information transfer. It is about changing thought or behaviour. If someone walks away with data in their head but no intention to act, you have not communicated effectively." [LG]
- "Without feeling an emotion, our audience will not care and will not change their behaviour." [B]
- "when you tell a story, you are trying to make an argument for an idea." [H]
- "Simplifying does not cost you authority. It increases comprehension and credibility at the same time." (Oppenheimer, 2005) [B]
- "Even informal meetings deserve preparation. What do I want this person to think or do differently when they leave?" [LG]
- No source uses the phrase "raise awareness"; the behaviour-change framing above is the nearest material.

## 2. Three types of language

- "Visual language helps our audience to imagine themselves in our world, emotional language engages them and logical language (precise dates, names, citations and sources etc) gives us credibility and authority. Make sure to use all of these types of language in your interview." [B]
- "Visual language begs us to see. Emotional language urges us to feel. Logical language urges us to believe." [LG]
- Visual test: "simply ask yourself: 'What does that look like?'" [H] Example: a distillery becomes "800,000 red bricks, a cathedral of light, 16 people per group, copper stills in the window". [LG]
- Emotional: "If you don't care about it, your audience won't either". [H] Routes: scenario, story, word choice and emoting; "That last one is the one people hold back, and it is usually the one that matters most." [B] Negative emotions "must be paired with positive emotions." [LG]
- Logical: "Specific numbers, brand names, people's full names and correct titles, years, places and so on." [H]
- Balance: "Statistics and detail lend credibility to the emotional and visual story that we want to tell." [B] In exercises, listeners "raise a hand if the speaker strays into abstract or non-visual language." [H] No scoring rule for balance exists in the sources.

## 3. Should Statements

- "A Should Statement is one sentence that captures what your work is ultimately fighting for. It names the problem, it reveals your values and it creates an emotional response, all at once." [B]
- Examples: "No child should be left alone in the dark." "Medical students should listen to their patients, they have so much to tell us." "A career in general practice should be sustainable." "People should have access to dedicated services to help them quit vaping." [B, LG]
- "Write yours, and use it to open. It tells the room why any of this matters before you ask them to care about the detail." [B]
- Introduction formula: "'My name is [X] and I believe that [Should Statement]. I work for [Organisation].' This transforms a mundane introduction into a declaration of purpose". [LG]
- "Scientific research is, by definition, an attempt to make the world better. The Should Statement is a way to name this clearly." [LG]
- It is the Problem in PIP: "What is wrong with the world as it stands?" [B]
- What makes a bad one: (no source material). The only tests available are the definition's three: names the problem, reveals values, creates an emotional response.

## 4. PIP, PEP, SCR and the Dandy Horse

- PIP: "PIP is the workhorse structure when you have something to show for the work." Problem (the Should Statement); Inspiration: "the thing you tried that nobody else did?"; Payoff: "Not your results, but what your results mean." "The payoff is the part people get wrong. Data is not a payoff. What the data changes for someone is." [B]
- PEP: "PEP is for when you do not have results yet, and it is about explaining a shift." Past: "This is how we used to do it". Event: "Then this happened, and it changed the picture." Present: "So now we have to do it differently." "It gives an audience a reason to be interested in work that is not finished, which is most work." [B]
- Note: the Handbook's PEP is "Pre-event, the Event, and Post-event", a personal story built on any event "as long as there is both change and emotion at its core." [H]
- "A story in business can be three sentences or three hundred." [H]
- SCR: (no source material).
- Dandy Horse: "If your work is one step in a long chain, tell the chain. The first bicycle was a wooden frame with no pedals, and it made every bicycle since possible." Framed that way "it stops sounding modest and starts sounding necessary." [B]
- When each applies (block labels): PIP "where the speaker has results"; PEP "Early stage work, or where the direction has changed and there are no results yet"; Dandy Horse "Incremental research, or anyone whose contribution feels small". [B] No source says "don't force-fit" outright.

## 5. Pyramid and the Content Tool

- "At the top sits the change you want: what people should think, feel or do. Underneath that, three messages. Underneath each message, three kinds of proof: a story, a statistic and a soundbite. That is 13 items in total". [B]
- Why: "Decide what you want people to think, feel or do after the interview or talk. Write it as one sentence." [CTG]
- Who: "who they are, what they care about and what motivates them. Then consider the barriers between you and them." [CTG]
- Watchout: "This is the section most people skip. It is also the most valuable to complete." "What is the one question I least want to be asked? Who is most likely to derail the room?" [CTG]
- Messages: "the three things that, if believed, would make the change you want inevitable. Three is the right number. Two feels weak. Four starts to feel like over-explaining." [CTG]
- "Always think of the message first, then the content that supports it." [CTG]
- Baker example: messages "freshest bread in Dublin, only natural ingredients, tastes extraordinary"; story "cycling to the mill at 4am"; statistic "45 minutes to make the dough, three ingredients"; soundbite "the freshest of the fresh". [LG]
- "Memorise only the soundbites and statistics, so that they roll off the tongue. Never memorise paragraphs." [CTG]
- "Even a quick completion before a meeting is far better than going in with nothing but instinct." [CTG]

## 6. Stories

- "Interesting stories are useful, but the best stories are ones that also fit your message." [B]
- "Your story should really not be any longer than 90 seconds". Cut filler: "great question, the way that I look at it is". [B]
- "A one-stop shop is a concept, not a story. A text at 11pm to a colleague who then signed a document on behalf of a client, that is a story." [LG]
- Ingredients: conflict, uncertain outcome, someone to root for ("Perfection alienates us; flaws draw us in."), beginning, middle and end, a higher purpose. [LG]
- "Real stories with emotion work best... (you can change the names if you have to). Give as much visual detail as possible." [B]
- "Don't memorise a personal story word for word. Remember the scene, the detail and the phrase you want to use". [B]
- Where to find them: why you got into the field, your first day in a lab or clinic, "the 95% of the time it is grinding and uncertain, and the 5% that keeps you going", patients. [LG] "you can always tell someone else's story." [H]
- "Begin with a bold statement or an intriguing question." Script the first and last line. [H]
- "Most people list the what and leave the why out, and the why is the part that makes it interesting and memorable." [B]

## 7. Statistics and numbers

- Numbers are "completely meaningless without context. An increase of 4% in sales, cancer rates or data breaches means nothing if we don't know the baseline". [H]
- "'240 million dollars spent on cancer prevention' tells us nothing unless we know the anchor, what it was last year, what it should be. Frame your numbers. Make them visual." [LG]
- "Use the minimum amount of data needed to make them believe your point." [LG]
- "One compelling statistic ('50% of heart failure patients will be dead within five years') is more persuasive than ten charts." [LG]
- "24% of the cohort saw a significantly decreased risk of aortic stenosis compared to placebo" becomes "roughly one in four patients had significantly better outcomes." [LG]
- Comparisons: "'two football pitches', 'would fill Wembley stadium' are fine, but they're a bit tired now as examples. Try to be original!" [H]
- Charts: "use as few data points as possible, give them context and be consistent with your x and y axes." [H]
- "One precise number that makes the point credible. Specificity is credibility." [CTG]

## 8. Soundbites

- "A short, memorable phrase. Rhyme and symmetry make it stick. Note down good lines when you hear them." [CTG]
- "people are more likely to agree with a statement that rhymes ('a stitch in time saves nine') than the same idea expressed in plain prose." [LG]
- "Soundbites work best when they visualise a thought or opinion... Making it personal works quite well. As do metaphors. Don't be afraid to plagiarise and adapt effective soundbites you may hear". [MT]
- "if you say it in a sharp and punchy way then it will be used prominently." A broadcast clip runs "15-20 seconds". [MT]
- Power of three: "use three examples, words, or ideas, not two (too few) or five (too many)." Symmetry: "loop back to the beginning in your closing line". [H]
- Collected examples: "Caring is a brutal gift"; "Every street has a story and every lane is a legend". [LG]
- "Good writing is lying on the floor. All we need to do is pick it up." [LG]

## 9. Metaphor and Brute Creativity

- "A metaphor maps something unfamiliar onto something the audience already knows. It is the fastest tool there is for explaining difficult work." [B]
- "If nothing comes to mind, force it. Pick a random object in the room and make it work. Most of what you produce will be rubbish, and then one of them will be the thing you use for the next ten years." [B]
- "Collect 3 random objects from your house. How is your work or project like each object? Force yourself to make a link". [H]
- Examples: "the cell as a city... the membrane as a wall, the mitochondria as a factory." An HIV researcher saw cytokine signalling in dominoes: "just like removing dominoes from the chain." [LG]
- For the invisible: "If it's invisible (systems, x-rays, bitcoin), use a metaphor". "Visual language is universal as long as you draw on shared imagery." [H]

## 10. Prisming

- "Prisming means shifting the central character of the story. The same piece of work looks different told from the point of view of the patient, the funder, the technician, the family, the person who will use it in ten years." [B]
- "Each perspective is a fresh entry point, which means one piece of work can give you five stories instead of one." [B]
- "This is an approach taken in newsrooms to keep a story alive over a number of days." List everyone affected "(directly and indirectly)", "be as diverse and far-reaching as possible", then "pick the most interesting person on your list and tell your story from their point of view." [H]
- "Instead of always telling the patient's story, tell the story of the person who cares for the patient. Or the colleague who swims with them on Sundays." [LG]

## 11. The Opposites Device / Opposite Effect

- "Open by saying the opposite of what the audience expects. It is risky, and when it works it buys you total attention for the next 30 seconds." "Use it once. It is a spice, not a meal." [B]
- Labelled for "Confident speakers looking for a stronger opening." [B]
- "What is the standard, predictable way that people typically talk about it? Now... what is the opposite of that? For example, why not try making a case for disorganisation or explain why sales should go down instead of up." [H]
- Opposites are a way of "finding humour and challenging current thinking. Shrek, Ratatouille and The BFG are all opposite-story films." [H]
- "Used carefully, with irony, satire, or provocation, this immediately wakes up a room." Examples: Lidl's "Lady Ball" provocation, then the reveal of its Ladies Gaelic Football support; Hans Brinker Budget Hotel: "It can't get any worse. But we'll do our best." [LG]

## 12. Suspense and foreshadowing

- "Do not lead with the finding. Set up the question first and let people want the answer." [B]
- "The information is the same either way. The difference is whether the audience is leaning in when they get it." [B]
- "Instead of: 'We found X. Here is how we did it', try: 'We did this work and something quite unexpected happened. But first, let me tell you what we were looking for...'" [LG]
- Mamet: "the only thing that keeps an audience in their seats is wondering what happens next." Hitchcock's bomb under the table: "Foreshadowing creates investment before you have explained anything." [LG]
- The Cut-Up: write your usual story as six sentences, scramble the order. "This is a great technique to build intrigue." [H]
- Exception for journalists: "Do not save your best point for the end of a careful build. Lead with it." [B]

## 13. Audience: barriers, persuasion, the room

- "Before you blame the message, check the barriers." Physical, Cultural, Language, Perception ("Who they think you are"), and "The messenger. Sometimes the right thing is for someone else to say it." [B] The Learning Guide lists six instead, adding Interpersonal and "The Perfection Trap". [LG]
- Name the sceptic: "I can almost hear the objections forming already, but I wanted to let you know that..." [B]
- Cialdini: "liking, reciprocity, authority, consensus, scarcity and consistency." "Usually one or two are doing all the work, and preparing for those beats preparing for all six." [B]
- "Spend 70 per cent of your time listening and 30 per cent talking." "you learn what someone objects to only if you give them the space to say it." [B]
- "what people say publicly in a meeting is often not what is privately driving their decision-making." [LG]
- Reading the room: "If it is not working, switch to empathy, reprioritise on the spot and decide the one thing you want them to leave with. If it is going well, that is the moment to make your ask." [B]

## 14. Thinking on your feet and Q&A

- Clarify: "'In what way?' or 'How do you mean?' is usually enough, and you often get a better version of the question back." [B]
- Repeat back ("So you're asking me if..."), delay ("I'm glad you brought that up."), bridge to familiar content. [B]
- "'I don't know, but I'll find out and get back to you by Tuesday' is often more impressive than bluffing." [LG] "Very good question, I haven't a clue! My area is really about..." [B]
- "The 3-part answer approach will always give you a structure: Past, Present, Future, or Micro, Macro, Global." [B] (The Knowledgebase says "personal/company/global".)
- "It's perfectly fine for you to pause before answering." [B]
- "The danger is almost never the question you prepared for, it's being surprised into an opinion." [B]
- "Identify with the emotion, in your own words, and then bring people to what is being done about it." [B]
- "Keep your replies short and offer to go deeper if they want it. 'Did that address your concern?' is a very effective sentence." [B]
- "Treat a difficult questioner as if they are genuinely curious." [B]

## 15. Media interviews

- Prepare once: a short synopsis "using visual language from the everyday world to explain the difficult parts", an FAQ, photographs, video. "Being informative is not enough, be interesting." [B]
- "You do not have to give the interview there and then." Ask about topic, programme, presenter, slot length, who else is on, and "May I have a copy of the interview for my records afterwards?" [B]
- "You cannot lie and you cannot really spin." Decide red lines in advance. [B]
- Blocking: "Look, there are some things that I can talk about, and some things that I can't, for obvious reasons. What I can tell you is..." / "There are some things that are commercially sensitive and this is one of those things. What I can tell you is..." / "You're a smart person, you know I can't tell you that. What I can tell you is..." / "We are working as fast as we can to find out the details." / "It would be irresponsible for us to take action without understanding the facts." / "You'll have to ask them, I'm not here to talk about that." [B]
- Bridging: "I will let you know as soon as I do." / "What we do know is..." / "From my point of view, the important issue is not so much x, it's rather y." / "You'll have to ask them, I can really only tell you about our position, which is..." [B]
- ABC: Answer, Bridge ("What I do think matters here is..."), Control (go to one of your three messages). "It works when it is done quickly, warmly and with a smile." [B]
- Radio and TV: "summarise in a visual and clear way in 30 seconds or so." On camera, "deliver to the interviewer rather than the lens unless you are told otherwise". On the phone, "stand up". [B]
- Print: "A print interview can run 30 minutes and produce three sentences of copy." "Stay switched on to the last handshake." [B]
- Delivery: "Smile, and use the presenter's name in your first answer." "Speak slowly and use descriptive language." "If the host starts nodding quickly, they are politely asking you to stop. An interview is a conversation, not a speech." [B]
- Avoid: "Never say 'no comment'. It is a red rag, and it becomes the story." Never criticise others: "You'll have to ask them, I'm here to talk about our own work." Assume off the record can be printed; beware "The walk back to the lift". Jargon like "scalable" and "paradigm shift". [B, MT]

## 16. His tone when coaching

- SESSION-BLOCKS.md has no separate style notes (nothing on "support and suggest" or "never patronise"). These points come from how his blocks and guides are written.
- Offer, don't dictate: "everyone has their own style. Please take what you find useful and leave the rest." [B]
- Assume they already have it: "You might think you don't have any stories right now, but I can tell you that you do, you just haven't collected them and worked on them yet." [H] Brand voice: "Warm and encouraging". [KB]
- Feedback order: "positive and then developmental". [LG]
- One fix at a time: "pick one thing to improve next time." [B]
- Normalise mistakes: "mistakes never seem as bad to the outside world as they do to you, and have a cup of tea." "Iteration is the whole game." [B]
- Respect the person: "make the person asking feel respected and intelligent." [B]
- Practical: concrete examples, plain sentences, humour and self-deprecation ("the Pratfall Effect in action"). [KB]
