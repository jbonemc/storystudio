Five Principles of Science Communication Online

What the evidence actually says about communicating research on social media.

1

Clarity Beats Density

One main message, everyday language, active voice. If the board of a major congress can't follow their own keynote, your audience won't either.

2

Visuals Must Clarify, Not Decorate

Infographics increase reach, but only when they do real explanatory work. A pretty graphic that hides caveats is worse than no graphic.

3

Narrative Structure Matters

Why does this matter? What was done? What should the audience take away? Three questions, answered quickly, every time.

4

Trust Comes From Cues

Show expertise, link to the source, name the study type, explain limits, and welcome questions. If you don't supply context, audiences infer it from weaker signals.

5

Uncertainty Needs Design, Not Avoidance

Uncertain messages get shared less, but transparency builds trust. State the uncertainty, translate it, pair it with what it means for action now.

Match the Message to the Platform

X / Threads

Real-time conversation

Lead with the finding, add method and limits in-thread. Use for paper launches, conference commentary, rapid expert exchange. Threads for lighter-weight, text-first updates.

LinkedIn

Professional impact

Explain why the work matters for practice, policy, or teaching. Strong opening paragraph, one clear takeaway. Build recurring series via newsletters. Reaches funders and collaborators.

Instagram

Visual explanation

Carousels: one slide, one idea. Reels for hooks plus one concept. Great for broadening audience beyond academia. Always add captions and alt text.

TikTok

Younger audiences

Open with the question or the misconception. Answer one point only. Use comments to source future content. Interactive, need-led formats maintain scientific integrity.

YouTube

Durable depth

Shorts for discovery, long-form for depth. Structure around audience questions. Invest in titles and thumbnails without clickbait. Caption everything.

Facebook

Communities & mixed-age

Pages for outward communication, Groups for community dialogue or course cohorts. Still essential where local relevance, events, or older audiences matter.

Talking Points

Slide 1 Five Principles of Science Communication Online

  • Open with the UEG anecdote: "I hosted the main stage at a gastroenterology congress — 10,000 people. After the keynote, I sat with the board. Over two-thirds of them said they didn't understand the presentation. These are experts in the field. If it happens there, it happens on social media ten times faster."
  • Clarity beats density applies everywhere — social media just compresses the stakes. You have seconds, not minutes. One message. Everyday language. If you can't say it in one sentence, you don't know it well enough yet.
  • Visuals: Infographics and carousels increase reach, but research shows they can also introduce spin if they skip the caveats. A visual abstract that looks beautiful but says "this drug works" when the study found "no significant difference" is worse than no graphic. The rule is: visuals should clarify your method and findings, not decorate them.
  • Narrative structure: This maps directly to what we covered in Session 1. PIP works just as well in a 280-character post — Problem, Inspiration, Payoff. "We asked [problem]. We found [inspiration]. Here's what it means [payoff]."
  • Trust cues: On social media, if you don't supply context, people infer credibility from aesthetics, follower counts, or whether it "feels right." You can beat that by doing four things: name the study type, link to the paper, state what you don't know, and invite questions.
  • Uncertainty: This is the hardest one. Research on 2 million tweets found that uncertain messages get shared less. So there's real pressure to strip caveats. But other studies show transparency builds trust. The solution isn't to hide uncertainty — it's to translate it. "This is observational, so it shows association, not cause. It still matters because..."

Slide 2 Match the Message to the Platform

  • The biggest mistake researchers make isn't choosing the wrong platform — it's forcing all communication into one platform's logic. An X thread, a LinkedIn post, and an Instagram carousel about the same paper should be three different pieces of communication.
  • X and Threads reward real-time conversation. Lead with the finding. Add method and limits in the thread. Great for conferences — live commentary puts you in front of the people in the room and the thousands who aren't.
  • LinkedIn is where funders, collaborators, and practitioners live. Don't post your abstract. Tell them why your work matters for their work. "If you're a clinician in [area], here's what this means for you." That's the post that gets shared to the right people.
  • Instagram and TikTok are about visual explanation and one idea at a time. Carousels: one slide, one point. TikTok: open with the question or the misconception, answer one thing, and use comments to source your next video. The TikTok research is clear — interactive, need-led content maintains scientific integrity better than scripted lectures.
  • YouTube is unique because content is durable. A 10-minute explainer keeps getting views for years. Use Shorts for discovery and longer videos for depth. And caption everything.
  • Facebook still matters — especially for Groups (course cohorts, local science communities) and mixed-age audiences. It's not fashionable, but if your audience is there, be there.
  • Key question to ask: "Who am I trying to reach, and where do they already spend time?" Not "which platform am I most comfortable on?"

Slide 3 From Paper to Post

  • This is where the Content Tool connects. The same thinking applies: what's the behaviour change? Who's in the room? What are my key messages? The only difference is the format is shorter and the audience is scrolling.
  • Walk through the 6-step workflow: Goal → Audience → Platform → Draft → Accessibility → Measure. This should become habitual. "I should post about this paper" turns into an editorial routine, not an afterthought.
  • "Before You Post" checklist — five quick questions. If you can't tick all five, it's not ready. The most forgotten ones are: naming the study type, adding alt text, and giving people a link to the source.
  • Common mistakes: The evidence is consistent — most poor social posts are written for an imaginary "general public" that doesn't exist. Name a primary audience and a secondary audience for every post. "Peers at my next conference" is different from "interested public who saw a headline."
  • Metrics: Likes and impressions tell you about distribution, not understanding. Saves and shares tell you about utility. Comments tell you whether people understood — or didn't. The right question is: "Did this post move someone from seeing my work to engaging with it?"
  • Accessibility is not optional: If your content isn't understandable with sound off, visuals unseen, or on a small screen, it's not ready. Alt text, captions, transcripts — build them in from the start, not as an afterthought.
  • Close with this: "Treat every social post as a public record of your professional judgement. That's not scary — it's empowering. It means you're building a body of clear, honest, accessible communication that people can find, trust, and share."
